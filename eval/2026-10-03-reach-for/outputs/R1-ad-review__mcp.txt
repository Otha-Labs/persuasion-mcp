I ran it through the persuasion-taxonomy diagnostic. It found **none of the nine questions a reader silently asks were fully answered**, and the craft score was 3.3/10.

**What works**
- The opener names a real pain, and "remote workers" tells readers who it's for.

**What's hurting it**
1. **There's no proof.** "Revolutionary" and "the best solution" have nothing behind them: no reviews, no numbers, no named feature. The ad also never says *how* the chair helps your back. Promising "all-day comfort" without evidence makes readers discount it.
2. **It sounds generic, even AI-written.** "Revolutionary," "unlock," and "It's not just a chair — it's an investment in…" were all flagged as stock phrasing. People scroll past copy they've seen a hundred times.
3. **Nothing lowers the risk.** Chairs are expensive, and buyers worry about fit and returns. There's no trial, return policy or price.
4. **The ask comes too soon.** "Shop now" lands before anything has earned belief. A first-person CTA like "Get my chair" usually beats it.
5. **It may break Meta's ad policy.** Meta's personal attributes policy rejects ads that suggest the viewer has a health condition, and "Tired of back pain?" is the kind of line that gets flagged. Check before you spend on it.

**A rewrite to work from** (the brackets need your real facts; don't publish invented proof):

> Hour six at the kitchen table, and you've lost count of how many times you've shifted in your seat.
> [Chair name] has [the one feature that's actually different, e.g. lumbar support that adjusts as you lean]. [Real proof: review count + rating, or a named customer quote].
> Sit in it for [30] days. If it's not for you, we'll pick it up free.
> **Get my chair →**

The opening line is an [In Medias Res Open](https://taxonomy.coppica.com/disrupt/in-medias-res-open): it drops the reader into a moment instead of asking a question. It also describes a situation rather than a medical condition, which avoids the policy risk.
